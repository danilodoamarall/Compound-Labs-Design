<Slide layout="title" kicker="State of Design 2026 · Part 3 of 5" title="The anxious middle" notes="This is the most delicate article in the series. Talk about structure, not about people.">

The people who code most with AI feel more valuable. Most of the profession is on the other side. This article is about them.

</Slide>

<Slide layout="chart" title="How will AI affect your role over the next two years?" kicker="Sentiment by role" notes="Read top to bottom: the roles are ordered by the vibe coding rate from article 1. The sentiment order is almost the same.">

<Chart name="outlook" />

I ordered the roles by the vibe coding rate from the first article. The sentiment order is nearly identical. **Design Engineers: 50% feel more valuable, 10.6% less secure.** **Researchers: 17.4% more valuable, 39.1% less secure** (n=23, directional reading).

</Slide>

<Slide title="The net, role by role" kicker="More valuable minus less secure" notes="The net is my own arithmetic: more valuable minus less secure. It is not a published figure.">

I did simple arithmetic: percentage saying “more valuable” minus percentage saying “less secure.”

- **Design Engineer: +39.4 points**
- **Lead / Principal: +23.2**
- **Manager / Director: +11.8**
- **Non-designer: +7.0**
- **IC Designer: −7.6**
- **Researcher: −21.7** (directional)

Among designers, the IC is the only one with a negative net. And the IC is **58.3% of the sample**.

</Slide>

<Slide title="The group that is the profession" kicker="Tension" notes="The central sentence of the article. Leave it alone on the screen.">

<Callout kind="tension" title="Tension · the anxious middle">

IC Designers run a negative net: 24.8% feel more valuable, 32.4% feel less secure. Design Engineers run a net of +39 points. The group that is the majority of the profession is the only one, among designers, with more fear than confidence.

The survey doesn't say whether adopting reduces anxiety or whether anxiety blocks adoption. But the correlation between the two curves is too strong to ignore in any conversation about a design career.

</Callout>

</Slide>

<Slide layout="chart" title="Satisfaction rises with the dose, step by step" kicker="Workflow satisfaction" notes="Every band scores above the one before it, without exception. The n-weighted average comes to 6.49, matching the published overall value.">

<Chart name="satisfaction" />

Satisfaction with your own workflow, 1 to 10. People who generate nothing with AI give it **5.93**. People who generate nearly everything give it **7.39**. A difference of **1.46 points**, and every intermediate step goes up.

</Slide>

<Slide title="Correlation, not cause" kicker="Caveat" notes="Be rigorous here. This is where the audience is most likely to overread.">

<Callout kind="caveat" title="Caveat · what satisfaction proves">

People who generate nearly everything with AI are 1.46 points more satisfied than people who generate nothing. That doesn't prove vibe coding causes satisfaction.

Self-selection explains a good chunk of it: the people who liked it kept going and are satisfied; the people who didn't, stopped. People who chose their own workflow tend to rate it better. Even so, the data does knock down the narrative that heavy adoption produces widespread frustration.

</Callout>

</Slide>

<Slide title="Two possible readings" kicker="Hypotheses" notes="Present both and say which one you think is more likely. Mine is the second, but they coexist.">

**Reading 1: adopting reduces fear.** People who try it discover that AI expands what they can do alone, and start feeling more valuable. The fear is fear of the unknown.

**Reading 2: fear blocks adoption.** People who feel threatened avoid the tool that represents the threat, and fall further behind. The fear is rational and self-confirming.

I lean toward the second, because it explains the seniority paradox from the first article. Lead and Principal have autonomy and job security; they can experiment without the experiment looking like a confession. The IC, measured on delivery, can't.

</Slide>

<Slide title="A management problem, not an individual one" kicker="Reading" notes="If you lead a team, this slide is for you.">

The useful question isn't “why doesn't the IC adopt.” It's **“what in the structure of the IC's work prevents it”**. Deadlines measured in screens delivered. No protected time to experiment. Tools that need security approval. No technical peer to ask without embarrassment.

None of those items is solved by an AI course. All of them are solved by decisions made by whoever organizes the work. And the cost of not solving them is having most of your team feeling like they're on the losing side of a transition that, by the numbers, the people who cross it rate as positive.

</Slide>

<Slide title="What the survey doesn't say" kicker="Limits" notes="Caveats.">

<Callout kind="caveat" title="Careful when citing">

The three sentiment percentages per role don't add to 100%; between 12% and 29% of answers fall into categories the aggregate extract doesn't carry, probably “don't know” or similar. Researcher has n=23. There is no published crosstab between sentiment and vibe coding band: the ordering is a strong coincidence, not a correlation measured per individual.

</Callout>

</Slide>

If you're an IC, pick a small prototype and generate the whole thing with AI this week, just to see what happens to your own net.
