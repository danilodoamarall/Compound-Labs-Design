<Slide layout="title" kicker="State of Design 2026 · Part 5 of 5" title="They complain about the excess, then plan for more" notes="Closing of the series. End with the local pulse survey proposal.">

The bottleneck in the 2026 design workflow is cognitive, not budgetary. And the profession complaining about too many tools is the same one planning to add more.

</Slide>

<Slide layout="chart" title="What gets most in the way of your workflow today?" kicker="Blockers" notes="The top three are 3.5 points apart and they're all talking about the same thing.">

<Chart name="blockers" />

**Time to learn tools (55.7%)**, **too many tools to evaluate (53.0%)** and **quality of AI output (52.2%)** are separated by 3.5 points. Budget (34.2%), security (28.9%) and engineering constraints (19.4%) come well after.

</Slide>

<Slide title="The bottleneck is cognitive" kicker="Reading" notes="The reading that organizes the article.">

The three biggest blockers are all talking about the same thing: **excess**. Too many things to learn, evaluate and review. It isn't a lack of money or of permission. It's a lack of attention.

That inverts a common assumption inside companies: that the brake on AI adoption sits in procurement or in security. For most of the designers surveyed, the brake is in the day itself: there's no time to learn the new tool, because there are three new tools a week to evaluate.

</Slide>

<Slide title="The stack contradiction" kicker="Tension" notes="The most ironic data point in the survey. Let the audience laugh, then think.">

<Callout kind="tension" title="Tension · they complain about the excess, then plan for more">

53.0% say there are too many tools. Over the last six months, 3.8% consolidated their stack. Over the next twelve, 17.5% plan to simplify, and 64.0% plan to add more AI coding.

The profession complains about the excess and, at the same time, plans to increase it. Whoever offers real consolidation, and not one more tab, has an entire market waiting.

</Callout>

</Slide>

<Slide layout="chart" title="How has your workflow changed in the last 6 months?" kicker="Change" notes="71.1% added AI or made it central. Only 3.8% consolidated.">

<Chart name="workflow" />

**71.1%** added AI or made it central in six months. Only **9.9%** say nothing changed. And **3.8%** consolidated tools: the smallest number in the entire survey.

</Slide>

<Slide layout="chart" title="Enterprise isn't stuck" kicker="By company type" notes="Startup and enterprise are 4.1 points apart. The ones falling behind are agencies and students.">

<Chart name="ai-central-by-company" />

The gap between startup (**38.8%**) and enterprise (**34.7%**) on “AI is central” is **4.1 points**. The narrative of the stuck large company is out of date. The ones falling behind are **agencies (28.6%)**, maybe because of the bill-by-the-hour model, and **students (21.7%)**, which suggests education hasn't caught up with practice.

</Slide>

<Slide title="What the survey can't tell us about Brazil" kicker="Here" notes="Be direct: the survey does not describe Brazil. That's an invitation, not a problem.">

South America came in with **49 responses (3.3%)**. Add Central America and the Caribbean and all of Latin America is **58 people, 3.9% of the sample**. That's enough to say the region took part. It's not enough to say anything about Brazil.

My bet is that here, two blockers weigh more than in the global average: **budget and procurement** (34.2% globally) and the dollar cost of every subscription. And that the “don't use” tribe from the first article is bigger. But it's a bet. The way to know is to ask.

</Slide>

<Slide title="A local pulse survey" kicker="Proposal" notes="A concrete proposal for the company. Ask for volunteers.">

<Callout kind="idea" title="Idea · asking here">

I propose running, inside the company, a pulse survey with the same core questions as State of Prototyping: vibe coding band, trust in the output, blockers, sentiment about the role and investment priorities.

With a hundred responses we can draw our own three-tribe bar and compare it to 37.7 / 31.2 / 31.1. The global data is the map; the local survey is the GPS.

</Callout>

</Slide>

<Slide title="What the survey doesn't say" kicker="Limits" notes="Caveats.">

<Callout kind="caveat" title="Careful when citing">

Blockers and priorities are multiple choice and add to more than 100%. The “added AI or made it central” grouping (71.1%) is my own sum of two answers. The startup versus enterprise comparison uses n of 433 and 262. There is no time series: “consolidated” could be rising or falling, and the dataset doesn't say.

</Callout>

</Slide>

<Slide layout="question" title="For the audience" kicker="Question" notes="Practical question, immediate answer.">

<Question>What would you drop from your stack tomorrow without losing anything?</Question>

**What to do on Monday:** take inventory. List every tool the team pays for or uses every week. Mark the ones that solve the same problem. Adopt a “one in, one out” rule for ninety days. And answer the pulse survey when it arrives.

</Slide>

<Slide title="What this series said" kicker="Closing" notes="Recap the five theses in one sentence each. End with the invitation.">

- **Three tribes, not a curve.** 37.7% at zero, 31.1% at majority, 31.2% in the middle. Two ways of working coexist.
- **Prototyping is not shipping.** 43.8% generate half or more; 1.4% trust it without review. Review became the craft.
- **The middle is anxious.** IC Designers, 58% of the profession, with a net of −7.6 points. A management problem.
- **Design system is the new API.** 40.2% invest in tokens, more than in canvas. It's the lever for quality.
- **They complain about the excess, then plan for more.** 53% say “too many tools”; 3.8% consolidated. Consolidation is empty.

All of it comes from a survey that doesn't describe Brazil. The next step is ours.

</Slide>
